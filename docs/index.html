The problem

Code ownership is subjective, political, and frankly emotional.

For decades, engineering organizations have relied on methods that depend on human judgment. OWNH removes the human element entirely.

Commit counts can be gamed.

Ten small commits outrank one good one. Volume is not stewardship.

Git blame only remembers the last person who touched a line.

Recency is not authorship. The original author deserves the credit, and the accountability.

CODEOWNERS files are fiction.

They are maintained by whoever lost the meeting, and they are out of date by the time they merge.

The method

Three steps. Zero ambiguity.

OWNH reads your history once and produces a single, reproducible answer for every line you ship.

Hash every line

Each line is reduced to its SHA-256 content hash, exactly as written. Whitespace is part of the line. A binary file is one line.

Find the first introducer

OWNH walks the complete history of every repository you provide and identifies the first contributor to ever produce each hash.

Assign ownership, permanently

Every occurrence of that line, in every file, in every repository, belongs to that contributor. Forever.

Research

Built on established methods.

OWNH takes proven ideas from software engineering research and applies them where no one else would.

  • First introducer as originStudies built on World of Code identify the repository that first committed each version of a file as its origin (Jahanshahi, Reid & Mockus). The Software Heritage archive answers "where does this file come from" with the oldest revision that contains it (provenance service). OWNH applies the same principle one line at a time.
  • Ownership predicts qualityIn Windows Vista and Windows 7, ownership measures correlated with both pre-release faults and post-release failures (Bird et al., "Don't Touch My Code!"). Ownership matters. OWNH makes sure it is assigned.
  • Line-based ownershipResearch comparing ownership measures finds line-based ownership suited to accountability and attribution, and finds that commit-based and line-based methods disagree about who the major developers are (Code Ownership: The Principles, Differences, and Their Associations with Software Quality).
  • Beyond the last authorgit blame shows the revision and author that last modified each line. OWNH reports the first.

FAQ

Questions, answered.

What happens when someone reformats the code?

Every reformatted line has a new hash, so ownership of those lines transfers to the author of the formatting commit. This is working as designed.

Does a blank line have an owner?

Yes. A blank line is a line like any other, and like any other line it has exactly one owner: the first person to commit one.

Is every closing brace really owned by one person?

One person per indentation level. },   }, and     } are different lines and may have different owners.

What if the owner has left the company?

Ownership is permanent. OWNH does not take employment status into account.

Can ownership be transferred?

Yes. Change the line.

What about copied code?

A copied line keeps its original owner, wherever it is pasted. If the line came from outside your organization, its owner may be someone you have never met.

Does OWNH support --ignore-revs-file?

No. A formatting commit is a contribution, and OWNH credits it accordingly.

Can OWNH estimate our bus factor?

Yes. For most lines it is one.

Can OWNH forecast ownership?

Yes. Every projection comes with an R².

What is a good code half-life?

Longer than your reorg cycle.

Who owns deleted code?

The person who wrote it first.
